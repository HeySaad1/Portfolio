Military Affairs / Army Organization

Army Organization

The organization of the military (jaysh) in Islamic governance is not merely an administrative function — it is a religio-political obligation whose structures, financing, and command are governed by Shari'ah principles extending back to the Prophetic era and the early Caliphate.

The Military Register — Diwan al-Jund

The Diwan al-Jund (military register) was established by the Caliph 'Umar ibn al-Khattab in 15–16 AH as a systematic record of all soldiers entitled to state stipends ('ata'). Its creation marked a pivotal transition from the informal tribal levies of the early Muslim community to a professional, bureaucratically administered standing force.

Each soldier enrolled in the Diwan received a fixed annual stipend drawn from the Bayt al-Mal, determined by seniority of Islam, genealogical proximity to the Prophet ﷺ, and service record. The Companions who participated in Badr received the highest allocations; subsequent cohorts were ranked accordingly.

Juristic Basis and Scope

Classical jurists grounded the obligation of maintaining a standing army in the Quranic command: "And prepare against them whatever you are able of power and of steeds of war by which you may terrify the enemy of Allah" (8:60). This verse is understood as imposing a collective obligation (fard kifayah) on the Muslim community, with the Imam bearing primary responsibility for its fulfillment.

Key Distinction: Jurists distinguished between the mutatawwi'un (volunteers) who fought without state stipend and the muqatilah (registered troops) on the Diwan. The former were bound only by the general obligation of jihad; the latter owed contractual duties to the state and could be disciplined for dereliction.

Administrative Structure of the Diwan

The Diwan al-Jund was organized by tribe and province under the Rashidun and Umayyad periods, with separate registers maintained for each garrison city (misr, pl. amsar). Major garrison cities such as Kufa, Basra, Fustat, and Damascus each had their own Diwan, administered by a designated secretary (katib) who reported to the provincial governor (wali).

Categories of Combatants

Islamic jurisprudence developed a nuanced taxonomy of those who participate in military operations, each category carrying distinct legal rights and obligations regarding stipend, booty, command authority, and accountability under the laws of war.

The Muqatilah — Standing Troops

The muqatilah were professional soldiers permanently enrolled in the Diwan al-Jund. They were obligated to respond to the Imam's call for mobilization, maintain readiness, and comply with military discipline. In return, they received fixed annual stipends ('ata'), land grants in some periods, and their families were provided for from the public treasury.

The Mutatawwi'un — Volunteers

Volunteers participated in specific campaigns without permanent enrollment. They shared in the distribution of war spoils (ghanimah) if present at the battle but received no state stipend. Their participation was governed by the general obligation of jihad rather than contractual duty to the state.

Auxiliary and Non-Muslim Forces

Classical jurisprudence addressed at length the permissibility of employing non-Muslim auxiliaries (ahl al-dhimmah) in military campaigns. The majority position, held by Hanafi, Maliki, and Hanbali jurists, permitted their participation when military necessity demanded it, provided they fought under Muslim command and their participation did not compromise operational security or the religious character of the force.

Command Hierarchy

The command structure of the Islamic military was conceived as an extension of political authority (wilayah), flowing downward from the Imam through appointed commanders. Each level of command carried both administrative and religious responsibilities, as the military was understood as an instrument of collective religious obligation.

The Imam as Supreme Commander

The Imam (Khalifah) held supreme command (wilayat al-harb al-'amma) over all military forces. This authority was inseparable from his political and religious mandate. Classical jurists held that the Imam personally leading the army was a meritorious act (mustahabb); his physical presence was associated with higher troop morale and demonstrated accountability.

In practice, as the Caliphate expanded, the Imam delegated operational command to appointed generals (umara' al-juyush), retaining supreme authority while entrusting tactical and operational decisions to military specialists.

The Amir al-Jaysh — Field Commander

The Amir al-Jaysh (army commander) exercised delegated authority over a specific expeditionary force or theatre of operations. His appointment was a formal act of delegation (tawliyah) from the Imam, and his authority extended to tactical decisions, discipline, the declaration of military truce at the operational level, and the distribution of captured wealth pending final accounting.

Sub-Commanders and Unit Structure

Below the field commander, the army was organized into units with designated sub-commanders (umara' al-arba', commanders of four): the vanguard (muqaddimah), right flank (maysarah — note: classical sources sometimes reverse these), left flank, rearguard (saqa), and centre (qalb). Each commander bore responsibility for the conduct of his unit under the laws of war.

The Principle of Obedience: The Prophet ﷺ commanded obedience to appointed commanders even if personally disagreeable, so long as they did not command disobedience to Allah. This principle — grounded in numerous authenticated hadiths — formed the juristic basis for military discipline and the legitimacy of command authority in Islamic law.

Conscription and Maintenance

The question of conscription — whether the state may compel military service — and the ongoing maintenance of soldiers engaged classical jurists extensively. The answers they developed reflect a careful balance between collective religious obligation, individual rights, and the fiscal capacity of the Islamic state.

Conscription — Fard Kifayah and Fard 'Ayn

Under normal circumstances, jihad in the sense of military service is a collective obligation (fard kifayah): if a sufficient number of Muslims fulfill it, the remainder are absolved. However, classical jurists held that the obligation becomes individually binding (fard 'ayn) in three situations: when the Imam formally conscripts specific individuals, when the enemy attacks Muslim territory and voluntary defenders are insufficient, and when an individual is personally present at the battle.

The Hanafi school recognized the Imam's authority to impose conscription on able-bodied free Muslim men in times of necessity (darura). The Maliki and Hanbali positions similarly affirmed this authority, grounding it in the principle that the preservation of the Islamic community constitutes one of the five essential objectives (maqasid) of the Shari'ah.

Military Stipends and the Bayt al-Mal

The maintenance of standing troops was the primary military expenditure of the Islamic treasury. Classical jurists held that soldiers on the Diwan were entitled to their stipends continuously — not merely when deployed — as they were considered permanently available for service. This principle effectively created a professional class whose livelihood depended on the state and whose loyalty was institutionally secured.

In periods of fiscal constraint, when the Bayt al-Mal could not sustain full stipends, jurists debated whether the Imam could reduce payments, require soldiers to supplement their income through trade, or grant land grants (iqta') in lieu of stipends — a practice that became widespread under the later Abbasid and successor states.

Exemptions and Military Service

The Quran and Sunnah specified categories of persons exempt from military service: those with physical disabilities, the blind, the lame, and those who are ill (48:17). Classical jurists extended these exemptions to include those with dependents who would suffer in their absence and those whose skills were more valuable to the community in other capacities — scholars, physicians, and essential craftsmen.

Historical Context: The Diwan system of 'Umar ibn al-Khattab transformed the Islamic polity's military capacity from a reactive, tribal mobilization model to a standing professional force. Later caliphs — particularly the Abbasids — further institutionalized this through the creation of specialized corps, including slave-soldier (ghulam) units, which eventually came to dominate Abbasid military structure by the 3rd century AH.
